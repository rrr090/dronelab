How it works

The project grew in three stages, and each one has its own test suite and a hand-written controller to beat.

1. Hover

One planar quadrotor holds its position under pushes and motor faults.

2. Cargo

Three 3D quadrotors share a load on elastic ropes. In a storm stronger than anything in training, the trained team succeeded in 75% of 8 test episodes and the baseline in 12%.

3. Mission

The team flies the cargo through a generated map to a goal pad. The score is the cargo's health on delivery.

The learned correction

Learning to fly from nothing scored below the baseline, so the network now starts from it. With a zero correction the team flies exactly like the baseline, and training only has to find improvements.

The first version of this also lost. With a correction three times larger, three training seeds scored 49, 78 and 69 against the baseline's 74, and the cargo was shaken apart by rope jerk. Shrinking the correction and keeping the best checkpoint on validation maps brought every seed to 75.5–76.8 on those same test maps. Because that choice was made while looking at those maps, later results come from new ones.

Rope jerk was still the main source of damage, so the network was given peak rope tension and past frames as inputs, plus a penalty that starts before damage does. All three seeds beat the baseline, by 6.2 points on average (95% interval +4.3 to +8.5). Both changes went in together, so this does not show how much each one contributed. Training then continued from the best model in rounds, and each round kept a new model only if it beat the old one on maps not used before. A doubled jerk penalty, a penalty on tension spikes and a critic that sees the wind state were each tested against continued training without them, and none of them clearly helped. The last two rounds trained on realistic hardware, described below. On the maps above, the policy shown here gains 2.8 points (95% interval +1.7 to +4.1) over the version released in v0.2.0.

Physics

Rigid-body quadrotors and a cargo on spring-damper ropes that can go slack and snap tight, written in vectorized NumPy.

Maps

Forest, city, gate, storm and mixed maps are generated from a seed. An A* planner finds a route wide enough for the formation.

Training

PPO written from scratch, with 64 parallel environments and a curriculum that ramps up wind, gusts and map density.

Cargo health

Starts at 100 and drops with rope jerks, high g-forces, impacts and ropes rubbing on obstacles.

Realistic hardware

By default the simulated hardware is ideal: motors respond instantly, commands act at once and sensors are exact. A stress test flies the same maps with 30 ms motor lag, a 20 ms control delay, sensor noise and bias, battery sag, motor wear, 1.25× wind, and payload and rope variation between flights. These values are plausible guesses for a small drone, not measurements.

Every policy loses a lot on realistic hardware, mostly through rope jerk. Stronger wind and slower actuation do far more harm together than apart. Removing the 20 ms delay from the full set of effects recovered 8.2 points, although the delay alone cost only 0.2. Training with these effects at a random strength per episode beat the same training without them by 1.8 points on realistic hardware (95% interval +0.7 to +3.1), with no loss on ideal hardware. A second round added 3.0 more (95% interval +2.4 to +3.6). Against v0.2.0, the policy shown here gains 10.2 points on realistic hardware (95% interval +5.4 to +14.9). The baseline was not retuned for delay, so its realistic score understates a tuned controller.

What the network learned

The analysis looks for neurons that track cargo swing, rope tension and obstacles, comparing each against untrained networks of the same shape. No single neuron beat them for swing or tension, and only a few did for obstacles.

These results are for the v0.2.0 policy, before realism training. Holding the top 32 tension-tuned neurons at their average while flying cost 2.3 points (95% interval −3.6 to −1.2) and raised rope-jerk damage from 5.8 to 8.0, against 0.7 points for each of two sets of neurons with similar influence. The model before it showed the same pattern, but v0.2.0 was trained from it, so that is not an independent replication. Swing neurons cost 1.9 points against no loss and 0.8 for their controls, which is suggestive but was not clear in the previous model. Obstacle neurons cost no more than their controls. This is one model on 60 maps with two control sets per concept, so it is evidence, not proof.

Limitations

This is a learning project, not a validated flight model. The cargo is a point mass, there is no aerodynamic interaction between drones, and the headline numbers use ideal hardware. The realistic-hardware effects are plausible guesses, not measurements. Results describe this simulator only.
